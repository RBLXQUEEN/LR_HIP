Section01

The task is to predict a number, not a category

Given a person's age, sex, body mass index, number of children, smoking status and region, estimate their annual medical insurance charge in dollars.

Because the output is a continuous quantity rather than a label, this is a regression problem — not classification. There is no probability and no threshold to cross; the model returns an amount, and we judge it by how close that amount lands to reality.

The practical reason this matters: insurance pricing is a decision where being wrong by a few thousand dollars per person, multiplied across a book of policies, is expensive in a way that a mislabelled image is not.

Say this

"The output here is money, not a yes-or-no. That single fact decides everything else — which model we use, which error metric makes sense, and how we judge whether it's any good."

Section03

The entire model is one equation

A linear regression has no hidden layers and no internal state. It is a weighted sum. These are the coefficients it learned from all 1,338 records:

ŷ = −2223.45
  + 263.62 · age       + 23.53 · bmi      + 516.40 · children
  − 500.15 · male      − 20415.61 · smoker
  − 585.48 · nw         − 1210.13 · se        − 1231.11 · sw
  + 1443.10 · (smoker × bmi)

Nothing was scaled, so each coefficient is in dollars per unit of its variable. Read the age term: one extra year of age adds $263.62 to the predicted charge, holding everything else fixed. That directness is the reason this model was chosen over a stronger black-box.

Three inputs are dummy variables — 0/1 switches. male is 1 or 0. Region has four categories, so it needs three dummies, and northeast is the baseline, encoded as all three being zero. The region coefficients are negative because they are measured relative to it.

The one term that matters most

The smoking terms only make sense added together:

cost of smoking  =  −20415.61  +  1443.10 × bmi

At BMI 32 that is −20,415.61 + 46,179.08 = +$25,763. The large negative base is a mathematical offset that lets the line pivot upward steeply with body mass — read alone it looks absurd, which is exactly why the interaction must never be interpreted in isolation.

This term is the difference between a mediocre model and a good one. Without it, the model assumes smoking adds a fixed amount regardless of weight, and R² is 0.784. With it, R² is 0.865.

Say this

"This is the whole model — nine coefficients and an intercept, in dollars per unit. The one I want you to look at is the last line. Smoking costs twenty thousand negative plus fourteen hundred per BMI point. Those two numbers are meaningless separately and only make sense added together. That term alone is the difference between explaining seventy-eight percent of the variation and eighty-six."

Section05

How good it is, and how I know

The coefficients were fitted by ordinary least squares — the values that minimise the sum of squared errors between prediction and reality. The closed form is β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, and that single expression is the training step.

0.865R² hold-out
$2,757Mean abs. error
$4,574RMSE
0.8375-fold CV R²

All four are measured on the 20% of records held back during training, so they describe performance on data the model has never seen. R² 0.865 means the model accounts for 86.5% of the variation in charges; the remaining 14.5% is individual difference these six inputs simply do not capture.

MAE $2,757 is the number to quote if someone asks how accurate it is in plain terms — a typical prediction misses by about that much. RMSE is higher at $4,574 because it squares the errors before averaging, so it punishes large misses harder; the gap between the two tells you the errors are not uniform, and a handful of people are predicted badly.

Cross-validation returns 0.837 against a hold-out of 0.865 — close enough to say the result is not an artefact of one lucky split.

Say this

"All four numbers come from data held back during training, so this is genuinely out-of-sample. If you only remember one figure, make it the mean absolute error — twenty-seven hundred dollars, which is a sentence a non-technical person can actually act on."

Section06

What this model cannot do

It is priced in the wrong country. These are US insurance charges in dollars. They reflect US medical pricing and tell you nothing about Indian premiums.

It knows no medical history. The six inputs are what a form captures. They do not include diagnoses, prescriptions, family history or claims — the things that actually drive real underwriting.

It assumes a straight line. The relationship between BMI and cost is almost certainly not linear forever. Outside the training range — BMI below 16 or above 53 — the model is extrapolating, and its answer there should not be trusted. One concrete example: the formula implies smoking becomes cheaper below BMI 14.15, which is nonsense, and nobody in the data is anywhere near that.

The data is small and observational. 1,338 rows is enough to demonstrate a method and not enough to build a pricing engine. The model describes correlation in this sample; it does not establish that any input causes the charge.

Closing line

"I'd rather state the limits than have you find them. Wrong country, no medical history, assumes a straight line, and a small sample. What it demonstrates is the method — and the method is what the project is about. Thank you."
